Quantum foundations: states, operators, measurement, spin

Everything in this course sits on five ideas: a state is a vector, observables are Hermitian operators, measurements give eigenvalues with probabilities |\text{amplitude}|^2, states evolve by the Schrödinger equation, and spin is the simplest place to practice all of it.

1. Why classical physics failed

Classical mechanics says a particle has a definite trajectory, any energy is allowed, and particles and waves are different things. Three experiments broke all three claims:

  • Blackbody radiation (Planck, 1900). Light of frequency \nu is emitted only in packets E=h\nu. Without this the classical (Rayleigh–Jeans) spectrum blows up at high frequency, the “UV catastrophe”.
  • Photoelectric effect (Einstein, 1905). Electrons leave a metal with E_k = h\nu-\phi. Brighter light gives more electrons, never faster ones; below threshold, nothing comes out. Light is made of photons.
  • Atomic spectra (Bohr, 1913). Atoms emit only sharp lines, h\nu=E_2-E_1 with E_n=-13.6\text{ eV}/n^2 for hydrogen. Energy is quantized.

Then the reverse surprise: matter behaves like waves. De Broglie: every particle has wavelength \lambda = h/p, so p=\hbar k and E=\hbar\omega. Davisson and Germer diffracted electrons off nickel. For an electron accelerated through V volts, \lambda\,[\text{Å}]\approx 12.27/\sqrt{V} (100 V gives 1.23 Å, the size of atomic spacings, which is why electrons diffract from crystals).

Two velocities belong to a wave: the phase velocity v_p=\omega/k (speed of a crest) and the group velocity v_g=d\omega/dk (speed of the envelope, i.e. of the particle). For a free particle \omega=\hbar k^2/2m, so v_g=\hbar k/m=p/m (the classical velocity) while v_p=v_g/2. You will reuse v_g=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE}{dk} for electrons in bands and v_g=d\omega/dq for phonons.

2. States are vectors: kets, bras, bases

A quantum state is a vector |\psi\rangle (a “ket”) in a complex vector space with an inner product, called a Hilbert space. Each ket has a dual “bra” \langle\psi|. The inner product \langle\phi|\psi\rangle is a complex number with these rules:

\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^*,\qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle\ge 0 .

Building a bra from a ket: if |\psi\rangle=\sum_j c_j|\phi_j\rangle then \langle\psi| = \sum_j c_j^*\langle\phi_j|. Every coefficient gets complex-conjugated. Forgetting this conjugate is the single most common lost point (the midterm solution even flags it).

Orthonormal basis: a set \{|\phi_j\rangle\} with \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}: each has length 1 (\langle\phi_1|\phi_1\rangle=1) and distinct ones are perpendicular (\langle\phi_1|\phi_2\rangle=0). In such a basis:

Components and inner products in a basis c_j=\langle\phi_j|\psi\rangle,\qquad \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\sum_j a_j^*c_j \;\;(\text{if }|\phi\rangle=\textstyle\sum a_j|\phi_j\rangle),\qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_j|c_j|^2 . A state is normalized when \sum_j|c_j|^2=1. Multiplying a state by an overall phase e^{i\alpha} changes nothing physical.

Think of it as ordinary vector algebra with one twist: the left vector gets conjugated. In column-vector language, |\psi\rangle \to (c_1,c_2,c_3)^T and \langle\psi|\to(c_1^*,c_2^*,c_3^*), a conjugated row.

A 3D Hilbert space has orthonormal basis |\phi_1\rangle,|\phi_2\rangle,|\phi_3\rangle.

(a) What are \langle\phi_1|\phi_1\rangle and \langle\phi_1|\phi_2\rangle? (b) |W\rangle=\gamma(|\phi_1\rangle+|\phi_2\rangle+|\phi_3\rangle) is normalized and \text{Re}\,\gamma=\sqrt3\,\text{Im}\,\gamma. Find \gamma. (c) Show that |\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|\phi_2\rangle+\frac{i}{\sqrt3}|\phi_3\rangle and |\theta\rangle=\frac{i}{2}|\phi_1\rangle+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|\phi_3\rangle are normalized. Normalization means \sum|c_j|^2=1. For (b) write \gamma=|\gamma|e^{i\varphi}: the ratio Re/Im fixes \tan\varphi.
For (e) conjugate the coefficients of the bra, then multiply matching components: \langle\psi|\theta\rangle=\sum_j\psi_j^*\theta_j.

3. Operators and their matrices

An operator \hat A turns a ket into another ket. Linear operators satisfy \hat A(a|\psi\rangle+b|\phi\rangle)=a\hat A|\psi\rangle+b\hat A|\phi\rangle. The simplest one is the outer product |\theta\rangle\langle\psi|: acting on |\chi\rangle it gives |\theta\rangle\,\langle\psi|\chi\rangle, i.e. the vector |\theta\rangle times the number \langle\psi|\chi\rangle.

Matrix elements. In a basis, A_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\hat A|\phi_j\rangle. Then \hat A=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}|\phi_i\rangle\langle\phi_j| and acting on a state is ordinary matrix multiplication of the column of coefficients. For an outer product, A_{ij}=\theta_i\psi_j^*.

Adjoint. \hat A^\dagger is the conjugate transpose: (A^\dagger)_{ij}=A_{ji}^*. Two special kinds:

Hermitian and unitary Hermitian \hat A^\dagger=\hat A: eigenvalues are real, eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are orthogonal. Every observable (position, momentum, energy, spin) is Hermitian, which is why measurements give real numbers.
Unitary \hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat 1: preserves lengths and inner products; eigenvalues are pure phases e^{i\lambda}. Time evolution \hat U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} and lattice translations \hat T_a are unitary.

Why Hermitian means real eigenvalues: if \hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle, then a\langle a|a\rangle=\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle=\langle a|\hat A^\dagger|a\rangle^*=\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle^* = a^*\langle a|a\rangle, so a=a^*.

With |\psi\rangle,|\theta\rangle,|W\rangle from the previous problem, define \hat A=|\theta\rangle\langle\psi|. (f) Write \hat A in terms of |\phi_i\rangle\langle\phi_j|. (g) Find \hat A|\phi_1\rangle. (h) Find \hat A|W\rangle. (i) Give all nine A_{ij}.

\hat A|\chi\rangle=|\theta\rangle\langle\psi|\chi\rangle: compute the number \langle\psi|\chi\rangle first, then multiply |\theta\rangle by it.

Coefficients: \theta=(\tfrac i2,\,0,\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) and \psi^*=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,\,-1,\,-i). Then A_{ij}=\theta_i\psi_j^*.

(f, i) A=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{i}{2\sqrt3}&-\frac{i}{2\sqrt3}&\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}\\[2pt]0&0&0\\[2pt]\frac12&-\frac12&-\frac i2\end{pmatrix}, i.e. \hat A=\frac{i}{2\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle\langle\phi_1|-\frac{i}{2\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle\langle\phi_2|+\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle\langle\phi_3|+\frac12|\phi_3\rangle\langle\phi_1|-\frac12|\phi_3\rangle\langle\phi_2|-\frac i2|\phi_3\rangle\langle\phi_3|. (Check one: A_{13}=\theta_1\psi_3^*=\frac i2\cdot\frac{-i}{\sqrt3}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}.)

(g) \langle\psi|\phi_1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}, so \hat A|\phi_1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|\theta\rangle=\frac{i}{2\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle+\frac12|\phi_3\rangle (the first column of the matrix).

(h) \langle\psi|W\rangle=\gamma\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}(1-1-i)=-\frac{i\gamma}{\sqrt3}, so \hat A|W\rangle=-\frac{i\gamma}{\sqrt3}|\theta\rangle=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt3}|\phi_1\rangle-\frac{i\gamma}{2}|\phi_3\rangle, with \gamma=e^{i\pi/6}/\sqrt3.

Show that every eigenvalue of a unitary operator is a pure phase e^{i\lambda} with \lambda real.

Take \hat U|u\rangle=\lambda|u\rangle. What is the bra of \hat U|u\rangle? Sandwich \hat U^\dagger\hat U=1.

Let \hat U|u\rangle=\lambda|u\rangle with |u\rangle\neq0. The bra of this equation is \langle u|\hat U^\dagger=\lambda^*\langle u|. Multiply the two: \langle u|\hat U^\dagger\hat U|u\rangle=\lambda^*\lambda\langle u|u\rangle. The left side is \langle u|u\rangle because \hat U^\dagger\hat U=1. So |\lambda|^2=1, which means \lambda=e^{i\lambda'} with \lambda' real. This result is reused in PS4 #1 to prove Bloch’s theorem: the translation operator is unitary, so its eigenvalue is e^{ika}.

4. Measurement: the postulates you actually use

  1. State: |\psi\rangle, normalized, contains everything knowable.
  2. Observables are Hermitian operators \hat A.
  3. Outcomes: a measurement of A can only return an eigenvalue a_n of \hat A.
  4. Probability (Born rule): P(a_n)=|\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2. For a wavefunction, |\psi(x)|^2dx is the probability of finding the particle in [x,x+dx].
  5. Collapse: right after getting a_n, the state is |a_n\rangle.
  6. Time evolution: i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle=\hat H|\psi\rangle.
Expectation value and uncertainty \langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_n a_nP(a_n),\qquad \Delta A=\sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2}. Two ways to get \langle A\rangle: weight eigenvalues by probabilities, or sandwich the matrix. Both must agree; the exams often ask for both.

The lecture also lists the composite-system postulate: two systems live in the tensor product space; states like (|0\rangle|1\rangle+|1\rangle|0\rangle)/\sqrt2 cannot be factored and are entangled. A mixed state is described by a density matrix \rho with \text{Tr}\rho=1 and evolves by i\hbar\dot\rho=[\hat H,\rho]. Know the definitions; they were not computed on PSETs.

5. Position, momentum, commutators, uncertainty

In the position representation, \hat x multiplies by x and \hat p=-i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}. A plane wave e^{ikx} is an eigenstate of \hat p with eigenvalue \hbar k. Operators need not commute; the commutator [\hat A,\hat B]=\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A measures that.

[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\quad\text{(act on a test function: } x(-i\hbar f')-(-i\hbar)(xf)'=i\hbar f\text{)}.

Non-commuting observables cannot both be sharp. The general uncertainty relation is \Delta A\,\Delta B\ge\frac12|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|, which gives Heisenberg’s \Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2. Physical picture from the slides: to localize a wave packet (small \Delta x) you must superpose many wavelengths, so p=h/\lambda spreads out.

Useful identities (prove them once): [\hat A\hat B,\hat C]=\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]+[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B; [\hat A,\hat B]=-[\hat B,\hat A]; [\hat x^n,\hat p]=in\hbar\hat x^{n-1}, i.e. [f(\hat x),\hat p]=i\hbar f'(\hat x).

Symmetry and conservation (Noether): time-translation symmetry conserves energy, space-translation symmetry conserves momentum, rotation symmetry conserves angular momentum. In a crystal only discrete translations survive, so only crystal momentum \hbar k is conserved, and only up to \hbar G. This sentence explains half of Module 5.

(a) Show [\hat A\hat B,\hat C]=\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]+[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B. (b) Using [\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar, prove by induction that [\hat x^n,\hat p]=in\hbar\,\hat x^{n-1}.

(a) Expand the right side and add and subtract \hat A\hat C\hat B. (b) Write \hat x^{n+1}=\hat x\cdot\hat x^{n} and use (a) with \hat A=\hat x, \hat B=\hat x^n.

(a) RHS =\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat A\hat C\hat B+\hat A\hat C\hat B-\hat C\hat A\hat B=\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat C\hat A\hat B=[\hat A\hat B,\hat C].

(b) Base case n=1: [\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar=i\cdot1\cdot\hbar\,\hat x^0. ✓ Inductive step: assume [\hat x^n,\hat p]=in\hbar\hat x^{n-1}. Then [\hat x^{n+1},\hat p]=[\hat x\hat x^n,\hat p]=\hat x[\hat x^n,\hat p]+[\hat x,\hat p]\hat x^n=\hat x(in\hbar\hat x^{n-1})+i\hbar\hat x^n=i(n+1)\hbar\hat x^n. ✓

\psi_0 is normalized with \langle x\rangle=x_0, \langle p\rangle=p_0. Let \psi_{\rm new}=e^{iqx/\hbar}\psi_0 with q real. Find (a) \langle x\rangle_{\rm new}, (b) \langle p\rangle_{\rm new}, and (c) interpret in one sentence.

|\psi_{\rm new}|^2=|\psi_0|^2. For momentum, apply -i\hbar\,d/dx with the product rule.

(a) |\psi_{\rm new}(x)|^2=|e^{iqx/\hbar}|^2|\psi_0|^2=|\psi_0|^2, so \langle x\rangle_{\rm new}=x_0.

(b) \hat p\psi_{\rm new}=-i\hbar\left(\frac{iq}{\hbar}e^{iqx/\hbar}\psi_0+e^{iqx/\hbar}\psi_0'\right)=e^{iqx/\hbar}(q+\hat p)\psi_0. So \langle p\rangle_{\rm new}=\int\psi_0^*e^{-iqx/\hbar}e^{iqx/\hbar}(q+\hat p)\psi_0\,dx=q+p_0.

(c) Multiplying by e^{iqx/\hbar} gives the particle an extra momentum q (a “kick” or boost) without moving where it is. This is why a Bloch function e^{ikx}u_k(x) carries crystal momentum \hbar k.

6. The Schrödinger equation and stationary states

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\hat H\Psi,\qquad \hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf r).

If V does not depend on time, try \Psi(\mathbf r,t)=\psi(\mathbf r)f(t). The equation splits into i\hbar\dot f=Ef, so f=e^{-iEt/\hbar}, and the time-independent Schrödinger equation \hat H\psi=E\psi, an eigenvalue problem. Its solutions are stationary states: |\Psi|^2 does not change in time.

Superpositions do move. If \Psi=c_1\psi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+c_2\psi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}, then |\Psi|^2 contains a cross term oscillating as \cos\big((E_2-E_1)t/\hbar\big). Every expectation value that couples the two states beats at \omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar, exactly the frequency of the photon for that transition. (Midterm Q1d.)

Boundary conditions. \psi is always continuous. \psi' is continuous wherever V is finite. Where V=\infty, \psi=0.

Probability current. J=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\psi'-\psi\psi'^*\right) obeys \partial_t|\psi|^2+\partial_xJ=0. For \psi=Ae^{ikx}, J=|A|^2\hbar k/m: density times velocity. This is why transmission coefficients carry a factor k_2/k_1 (see PS1 #2 in Module 2).

Schrödinger vs diffusion (slides): free-particle Schrödinger is \partial_t\psi=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\psi, a diffusion equation with imaginary diffusion constant. Diffusion damps Fourier components as e^{-Dk^2t}; Schrödinger only rotates their phases e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}, so information is preserved (unitary). Both spread a Gaussian, for different reasons. Replacing t\to-i\tau (imaginary time) turns one into the other.

7. Spin ½: the two-level system you must master

An electron has an intrinsic angular momentum with only two outcomes along any axis: \pm\hbar/2. Use the basis |\uparrow\rangle=\binom10, |\downarrow\rangle=\binom01 (eigenstates of \hat S_z). With \hat{\mathbf S}=\frac\hbar2\vec\sigma:

\hat S_x=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \hat S_y=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \hat S_z=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
Eigenvectors to memorize S_z: |\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\rangle. \;S_x=\pm\frac\hbar2: \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom{1}{\pm1}. \;S_y=\pm\frac\hbar2: \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom{1}{\pm i}.
For |\chi\rangle=\binom{a}{b} normalized: \langle S_z\rangle=\frac\hbar2(|a|^2-|b|^2), \;\langle S_x\rangle=\hbar\,\text{Re}(a^*b), \;\langle S_y\rangle=\hbar\,\text{Im}(a^*b).

Spin in a magnetic field. With \hat H=\omega_0\hat S_z, |\uparrow\rangle has energy +\hbar\omega_0/2 and |\downarrow\rangle has -\hbar\omega_0/2. Each component picks up its own phase: \binom ab\to\binom{a\,e^{-i\omega_0t/2}}{b\,e^{+i\omega_0t/2}}. |a|,|b| never change (so \langle S_z\rangle is constant) but the relative phase rotates at \omega_0, so \langle S_x\rangle,\langle S_y\rangle rotate: Larmor precession about z with period 2\pi/\omega_0=h/(\hbar\omega_0).

1. The infinite square well, derived slowly

Setup. V=0 for 0\lt x\lt L, V=\infty elsewhere.

Step 1, outside. In \hat H\psi=E\psi the term V\psi would be infinite unless \psi=0. So \psi=0 outside, and continuity of \psi forces \psi(0)=\psi(L)=0. (We do not require \psi' continuous at an infinite wall.)

Step 2, inside. -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''=E\psi, i.e. \psi''=-k^2\psi with k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar. General solution \psi=A\sin kx+B\cos kx.

Step 3, boundary conditions. \psi(0)=B=0. \psi(L)=A\sin kL=0 with A\ne0 ⇒ kL=n\pi.

Step 4, which n? n=0 gives \psi\equiv0 (no particle). Negative n give -\psi_n, the same state up to a phase. So n=1,2,3,\dots

Step 5, normalize. \int_0^LA^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)dx=A^2L/2=1 ⇒ A=\sqrt{2/L}.

Infinite well results \psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m}=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}=\frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}. E_n\propto n^2, E_n\propto1/L^2, E_n\propto1/m. \psi_n has n-1 interior nodes; \psi_1,\psi_3 are even about the center, \psi_2 odd.

Why the ground state energy is not zero. Confining the particle to width L makes \Delta x\sim L, so \Delta p\gtrsim\hbar/L and kinetic energy \sim(\Delta p)^2/2m\sim\hbar^2/mL^2\neq0. The zero-point energy is the uncertainty principle in action.

A particle at rest at a definite spot would have \Delta p=0 and \Delta x\lt L, violating \Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2; confinement to L forces \Delta p\gtrsim\hbar/2L and a nonzero kinetic energy.

3. The 3D box and degeneracy

For V=0 inside a cube of side L, try \psi=X(x)Y(y)Z(z). Dividing the Schrödinger equation by XYZ separates it into three 1D wells: -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{X''}{X}=E_x, etc., with E=E_x+E_y+E_z. So

\psi=\left(\tfrac2L\right)^{3/2}\sin\tfrac{n_x\pi x}{L}\sin\tfrac{n_y\pi y}{L}\sin\tfrac{n_z\pi z}{L},\qquad E=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}(n_x^2+n_y^2+n_z^2),\quad n_i\ge1 .

4. Superposition dynamics and selection rules

Put the electron in \Psi=\frac1{\sqrt2}(\psi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\psi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}). Because \psi_{1,2} are real,

|\Psi|^2=\tfrac12\left[\psi_1^2+\psi_2^2+2\psi_1\psi_2\cos\omega_{21}t\right],\qquad\omega_{21}=\frac{E_2-E_1}{\hbar}.

So \langle z\rangle(t)=\frac L2+z_{12}\cos\omega_{21}t with z_{12}=\int\psi_1z\psi_2\,dz=-\frac{16L}{9\pi^2}. The charge sloshes back and forth: an oscillating dipole, which radiates or absorbs at f=(E_2-E_1)/h, the photon frequency.

Parity selection rule. Light couples through eEz, so a transition i\to f needs z_{fi}=\int\psi_fz\psi_i\neq0. About the well center, \psi_n(L-z)=(-1)^{n+1}\psi_n(z): odd n even, even n odd. Write z=\frac L2+(z-\frac L2). The \frac L2 part gives \frac L2\langle\psi_f|\psi_i\rangle=0 (orthogonal), and (z-\frac L2) is odd, so the integral survives only when \psi_i\psi_f is odd: the states must have opposite parity. 1\to2 allowed, 1\to3 forbidden.

5. The quantum harmonic oscillator

V=\frac12m\omega^2x^2 (a spring, k=m\omega^2). Classically any energy \frac12kA^2 is allowed and the particle can sit at rest at x=0. Quantum mechanically that would mean \Delta x=\Delta p=0, so the minimum energy cannot be zero.

Oscillator results E_n=\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots\qquad \psi_0=\left(\tfrac{1}{\pi b^2}\right)^{1/4}e^{-x^2/2b^2},\quad b=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}. Equal spacing \hbar\omega (unlike the box’s n^2). \psi_n=N_ne^{-x^2/2b^2}H_n(x/b) with Hermite polynomials H_0=1, H_1=2\xi, H_2=4\xi^2-2. Ladder operators: \hat H=\hbar\omega(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12), [\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1, \hat a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle, \hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle.

Where quantization comes from here (lecture derivation): substituting \psi=e^{-\alpha x^2/2}H gives Hermite’s equation. A power-series solution generally grows like e^{+x^2} and blows up, unless the series terminates. Termination requires \lambda/\alpha=2n+1, which is exactly E_n=(n+\frac12)\hbar\omega.

Why it matters later: every lattice vibration mode is one of these oscillators; its quantum \hbar\omega is a phonon (Module 10).

6. Potential steps, scattering and tunneling

Where E>V the solution oscillates: e^{\pm ikx} with k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar. Where E\lt V it decays or grows: e^{\pm\kappa x} with \kappa=\sqrt{2m(V-E)}/\hbar. A wave traveling right is e^{+ikx} (with e^{-i\omega t}); left is e^{-ikx}.

Reflection and transmission For incident \alpha e^{ik_1x}, reflected \beta e^{-ik_1x}, transmitted \gamma e^{ik_2x}, compare currents J=|A|^2\hbar k/m: R=\frac{|\beta|^2}{|\alpha|^2},\qquad T=\frac{k_2}{k_1}\frac{|\gamma|^2}{|\alpha|^2},\qquad R+T=1. The factor k_2/k_1 is there because the transmitted wave moves at a different speed.

Tunneling: through a barrier of height V_0>E and width b, the wave decays as e^{-\kappa x}, so T\sim e^{-2\kappa b}. This is why the Kronig–Penney barriers let electrons hop between wells (bands have width) and why infinitely strong barriers isolate them (bands collapse to levels).

Crystal lattices and crystal structures

A crystal is a pattern repeated forever. To describe it you need two things: the grid of repeat points (the lattice) and what sits at each point (the basis). Everything else (unit cells, packing, Miller indices) is bookkeeping on top of that, and it is pure points on the exam.

1. Lattice + basis = crystal structure

A Bravais lattice is an infinite array of points generated by three primitive vectors:

\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3,\qquad n_i\in\mathbb Z .

The defining property: the arrangement and the orientation of the surroundings look exactly the same from every lattice point. A periodic set of points that fails this (some points see neighbors on the left, others on the right) is not a Bravais lattice; it is a Bravais lattice with a multi-point basis.

The basis is the atom or group of atoms attached identically to every lattice point. Crystal structure = lattice + basis. The basis does not change the lattice, so it does not change the reciprocal lattice or the Brillouin zone (Module 4); it changes diffraction intensities and the number of bands/phonon branches.

Counting: 5 Bravais lattices in 2D (oblique, rectangular, centered rectangular, square, hexagonal); 14 in 3D grouped into 7 crystal systems (cubic: sc, bcc, fcc; tetragonal: 2; orthorhombic: 4; hexagonal: 1; trigonal: 1; monoclinic: 2; triclinic: 1).

Honeycomb (graphene) is not a Bravais lattice. Take two neighboring carbon atoms A and B: A has its three bonds pointing, say, up-left, up-right and down, while B’s point down-left, down-right and up. Same distances, rotated environment ⇒ not equivalent. It is the triangular (hexagonal) Bravais lattice with a 2-atom basis.

2. Unit cells, primitive cells, Wigner–Seitz

  • Unit cell: any volume that fills all space when translated by some set of lattice vectors, without gaps or overlaps. Not unique.
  • Primitive cell: a unit cell containing exactly one lattice point. Its volume is V_c=|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)| whatever shape you choose.
  • Conventional cell: a bigger, more symmetric cell (the cube for bcc and fcc), containing several points.

Counting points in a cell: corner = 1/8 (shared by 8 cells), edge = 1/4, face = 1/2, interior = 1. In 2D: corner = 1/4, edge = 1/2.

Wigner–Seitz cell recipe 1. Pick a lattice point. 2. Draw lines to its nearest neighbors (then next-nearest, …). 3. Draw the perpendicular bisector (plane in 3D) of each line. 4. The smallest region enclosed around the point is the WS cell.
It is always primitive: every point in space belongs to the WS cell of the lattice point closest to it, so the cells tile space and each holds exactly one point. It has the full symmetry of the lattice. Do the same construction in reciprocal space and you get the first Brillouin zone.

4. Diamond, zinc blende and other structures with a basis

Diamond (C, Si, Ge): fcc lattice + 2-atom basis at (0,0,0) and \frac a4(1,1,1). Equivalently, two fcc sublattices shifted by a quarter of the body diagonal. Each atom has 4 nearest neighbors at the corners of a tetrahedron (sp³ bonds).

  • Atoms per conventional cube: 4\times2=8. Si: 8/a^3=8/(5.431\times10^{-8}\text{ cm})^3=5.0\times10^{22} cm^{-3}.
  • Nearest-neighbor distance: |\frac a4(1,1,1)|=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a (Si: 2.35 Å). Coordination 4. Packing fraction only 0.34: a very open structure.
  • Atoms per primitive cell: 2. This “2” is why Si has 2 atoms/primitive cell for counting bands (Module 5) and phonon branches (6 = 3 acoustic + 3 optical, Module 10).

Zinc blende (GaAs, ZnS, InP): the same geometry, but the two basis atoms are different species. Same-species neighbors (Zn–Zn) are fcc nearest neighbors at a/\sqrt2. Losing the identical-atom symmetry removes the inversion center, which matters for spin splitting (Module 5).

Others you should recognize: rock salt NaCl (fcc + Na at 0, Cl at \frac a2(1,0,0)); CsCl (sc + 2-atom basis, not bcc!); hcp (hexagonal + 2 atoms); graphene (triangular + 2 atoms, a=0.246 nm).

2. Take reciprocals (1/\infty=0).
3. Multiply by the smallest factor that makes them integers. Negative indices get a bar: (\bar1 10).

Reciprocal lattice, Brillouin zones and X-ray diffraction

Electrons, X-rays and phonons in a crystal are waves, labeled by wavevectors. The reciprocal lattice is the set of wavevectors that “fit” the crystal’s periodicity. It tells you where X-rays diffract, where band gaps open, and how many electron states a band holds.

1. Why we need a second space

Anything with the crystal’s periodicity, like the potential V(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=V(\mathbf r) or the electron density, can be written as a Fourier series V(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf G}V_{\mathbf G}e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}. For each term to have the lattice period we need e^{i\mathbf G\cdot(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)}=e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}, i.e. e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1 for every lattice vector. Those special wavevectors \mathbf G form the reciprocal lattice. In 1D with spacing a: G=2\pi n/a. Small real-space spacing ⇒ large reciprocal spacing (it is a Fourier transform).

2. Definition and construction

Reciprocal lattice e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1\iff\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R=2\pi m,\qquad \mathbf G=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3, \mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)},\quad\mathbf b_2=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1}{V_c},\quad\mathbf b_3=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2}{V_c},\qquad \mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}. Reciprocal primitive volume =(2\pi)^3/V_c. \ \mathbf G_{hkl}\perp(hkl) and |\mathbf G_{hkl}|=2\pi/d_{hkl}.

2D shortcut: solve \mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j=2\pi\delta_{ij} directly. \mathbf b_1 must be perpendicular to \mathbf a_2, \mathbf b_2 perpendicular to \mathbf a_1.

Why \mathbf G_{hkl}\perp(hkl): the plane (hkl) passes through \mathbf a_1/h, \mathbf a_2/k, \mathbf a_3/l, so it contains \mathbf a_1/h-\mathbf a_2/k and \mathbf a_1/h-\mathbf a_3/l. Dot with \mathbf G: 2\pi-2\pi=0 for both. The plane’s distance from the origin is \frac{\mathbf a_1}{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf G}=\frac{2\pi}{|\mathbf G|}, so d_{hkl}=2\pi/|\mathbf G_{hkl}|.

4. Brillouin zones

First Brillouin zone The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice: all \mathbf k closer to \mathbf G=0 than to any other \mathbf G. Its boundaries are the perpendicular bisector planes of the \mathbf G’s.
1D: -\pi/a\lt k\le\pi/a. Square lattice: square of side 2\pi/a. bcc lattice: rhombic dodecahedron (WS of fcc). fcc lattice (Si, GaAs, Cu): truncated octahedron (WS of bcc).
Volume =(2\pi)^3/V_c, the same as any reciprocal primitive cell, independent of which real-space primitive cell you started from (all have volume V_c).

Why the BZ is special: a wave with \mathbf k on a zone boundary satisfies |\mathbf k+\mathbf G|=|\mathbf k| for some \mathbf G: squaring, 2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G+G^2=0, i.e. \mathbf k\cdot(-\hat{\mathbf G})=|\mathbf G|/2, which is the perpendicular bisector of -\mathbf G. Those waves are Bragg-reflected into states of equal energy. That degeneracy is what a weak potential splits into a band gap (Module 5).

The basis does not change the zone. Si (diamond) has the fcc lattice, so its BZ is the fcc truncated octahedron. High-symmetry points of the fcc zone: \Gamma=0; X=\frac{2\pi}a(1,0,0); L=\frac\pi a(1,1,1); K=\frac{2\pi}a(\frac34,\frac34,0). Si’s conduction minima lie near X (along Δ); GaAs’s lie at Γ.

5. X-ray diffraction: Bragg’s law is the Laue condition

X-ray wavelengths (Cu Kα: 1.5406 Å) match plane spacings (1–5 Å). A beam with \mathbf k_{\rm in} scatters elastically into \mathbf k_{\rm out}, |\mathbf k_{\rm in}|=|\mathbf k_{\rm out}|=k=2\pi/\lambda.

Bragg (real space) ⇔ Laue (k-space) Bragg: 2d\sin\theta=n\lambda, with \theta the glancing angle measured from the planes, so the beam is deflected by 2\theta (what a diffractometer plots).
Scattering vector: |\mathbf q|=|\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}|=2k\sin\theta=\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta.
Laue: diffraction happens when \mathbf q=\mathbf G.
Cubic, first order: \sin^2\theta=\dfrac{\lambda^2}{4a^2}(h^2+k^2+l^2).

Proof that they are the same. Magnitude: |\mathbf q|=\frac{4\pi}\lambda\sin\theta=\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{2d}=\frac{2\pi}{d}=|\mathbf G|. Direction: the planes act like a mirror, so \mathbf k_{\rm in},\mathbf k_{\rm out} make equal angles with them; their in-plane components cancel in \mathbf q, leaving \mathbf q along the plane normal, which is the direction of \mathbf G. Order n ⇒ \mathbf q=n\mathbf G, also a reciprocal lattice vector. Derivation of |\mathbf q|: q^2=2k^2-2k^2\cos2\theta=4k^2\sin^2\theta.

In a powder pattern, small \theta means large d: rings near the center come from the widest-spaced planes.

6. Why some peaks are missing (structure factor)

The amplitude scattered by one cell adds waves from every atom j at \mathbf r_j: S_{\mathbf G}=\sum_jf_je^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j}. Using conventional-cube indices, \mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j=2\pi(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j) for fractional coordinates.

  • fcc (atoms at 0, (0\frac12\frac12), (\frac120\frac12), (\frac12\frac120)): S=f[1+e^{i\pi(k+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+k)}] =4f if h,k,l all even or all odd, else 0. So (100), (110), (210) vanish; (111), (200), (220), (311) survive.
  • bcc (atoms at 0 and (\frac12\frac12\frac12)): S=f[1+e^{i\pi(h+k+l)}] ⇒ allowed only if h+k+l is even: (110), (200), (211), (220).

1. Bloch’s theorem, derived from symmetry

Setup. V(x+a)=V(x). Define the translation operator \hat T_a\psi(x)=\psi(x+a).

  1. \hat T_a commutes with \hat H. \hat T_a[\hat H\psi](x)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x+a)+V(x+a)\psi(x+a)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x+a)+V(x)\psi(x+a)=\hat H[\hat T_a\psi](x). Commuting operators share eigenstates, so we can choose energy eigenstates with \psi(x+a)=\lambda\psi(x).
  2. |\lambda|=1. \hat T_a is unitary (shifting preserves \int|\psi|^2), and unitary eigenvalues are pure phases (PS1 #4). Also physically: if |\lambda|\ne1, |\psi| would grow without bound in one direction and could not be normalized.
  3. Born–von Kármán ring. Join N cells in a ring: \psi(x+Na)=\psi(x) ⇒ \lambda^N=1 ⇒ \lambda=e^{ika} with k=\dfrac{2\pi j}{Na}, j integer.
  4. Periodic part. Define u(x)=e^{-ikx}\psi(x). Then u(x+a)=e^{-ik(x+a)}e^{ika}\psi(x)=u(x).
Bloch’s theorem \psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\quad u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r). Consequences: |\psi|^2 has the lattice period, so the electron is spread equally over every cell (delocalized). \mathbf k and \mathbf k+\mathbf G label the same states, so restrict \mathbf k to the first BZ (reduced zone) and add a band index n. \hbar\mathbf k is crystal momentum: conserved only modulo \hbar\mathbf G, and not the eigenvalue of \hat p because u_{\mathbf k} contains many plane waves e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}.

2. Counting states: why some crystals are metals

Bands from atoms (tight-binding picture, slide 36). Two identical atoms brought together split each level into bonding and antibonding. N atoms split each atomic level into N closely spaced levels: a band, of width set by the overlap. Deep core levels overlap little and stay narrow; valence levels broaden into wide bands and may overlap.

The electron-counting rule Each band holds 2 electrons per primitive cell. A completely full band carries no current (every +k is matched by -k). So:
• Odd number of valence electrons per primitive cell ⇒ some band is half-filled ⇒ metal.
• Even number ⇒ can fill bands exactly ⇒ insulator/semiconductor if bands do not overlap; if they overlap in energy, still a metal (Ca, Mg).

Insulator vs semiconductor is only gap size: Si 1.12 eV, Ge 0.66 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV, diamond 5.5 eV. A material is transparent to photons with h\nu\lt E_g; visible light is 1.8–3.1 eV.

4. Nearly free electrons: how a weak potential opens a gap

Bragg reflection at the zone boundary. Elastic Bragg scattering by G needs |k-G|=|k|, i.e. k=G/2=n\pi/a: exactly the zone boundaries. There, e^{i\pi x/a} and e^{-i\pi x/a} have equal energy and the lattice scatters one into the other, so the true eigenstates are standing waves:

\psi_+\propto\cos\frac{\pi x}a\ \ (\text{charge piled on the ions at }x=na),\qquad\psi_-\propto\sin\frac{\pi x}a\ \ (\text{charge between the ions}).

Electrons near the (attractive) ion cores have lower potential energy, so \psi_+ sits lower and \psi_- higher. Same k, different energies ⇒ a gap.

Degenerate perturbation theory at the boundary (discussion 4) In the 2-state basis \phi_1=e^{i\pi x/a}/\sqrt L, \phi_2=e^{-i\pi x/a}/\sqrt L with V(x)=\sum_GV_Ge^{iGx}: H=\begin{pmatrix}E_0+V_0&V_G\\V_G^*&E_0+V_0\end{pmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ E_\pm=E_0+V_0\pm|V_G|,\qquad E_g=2|V_G| . Near the boundary, with \varepsilon_k^0 and \varepsilon_{k-G}^0 the two free energies: E_\pm(k)=\frac{\varepsilon^0_k+\varepsilon^0_{k-G}}2\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon^0_k-\varepsilon^0_{k-G}}2\right)^2+|V_G|^2}. The gap equals twice the Fourier component of the potential that connects the two waves. Away from degeneracies, ordinary second-order perturbation theory gives only small shifts.

5. The Kronig–Penney model

An exactly solvable periodic potential: wells 0\lt x\lt a (U=0) and barriers -b\lt x\lt0 (U=U_0), period a+b, energy 0\lt\varepsilon\lt U_0.

Recipe. (1) Well: \psi=Ae^{iKx}+Be^{-iKx}, \varepsilon=\hbar^2K^2/2m. Barrier: \psi=Ce^{Qx}+De^{-Qx}, U_0-\varepsilon=\hbar^2Q^2/2m. (2) Continuity of \psi and \psi' at x=0. (3) At x=a the next barrier is related to the previous one by Bloch: \psi(a\lt x\lt a+b)=e^{ik(a+b)}\psi(x-a-b). (4) Four homogeneous equations; nontrivial solution ⇒ determinant =0:

\frac{Q^2-K^2}{2QK}\sinh Qb\,\sin Ka+\cosh Qb\cos Ka=\cos k(a+b).

Delta-function limit. b\to0, U_0\to\infty with P=Q^2ba/2 fixed. Then Qb\to0 (Q\sim b^{-1/2}), so \sinh Qb\to Qb, \cosh Qb\to1, Q^2\gg K^2 and \frac{Q^2}{2QK}Qb=\frac{Q^2b}{2K}=\frac{P}{Ka}:

Kronig–Penney (delta barriers) \frac{P}{Ka}\sin Ka+\cos Ka=\cos ka . The right side lies in [-1,1] for real k. Energies (values of Ka) where the left side exceeds 1 in magnitude have no real k: gaps. At Ka=n\pi the left side is \cos n\pi=\pm1 for any P ⇒ ka=0 or \pi ⇒ every band ends at Ka=n\pi, at the zone center or edge. P measures barrier strength: P\to\infty shrinks bands to the infinite-well levels Ka=n\pi; P\to0 recovers free electrons.

6. Tight binding: the opposite limit

Start from isolated atomic orbitals \phi(x-na) and let neighbors overlap weakly. The Bloch combination \psi_k=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_ne^{ikna}\phi(x-na) with nearest-neighbor hopping t gives

1D tight-binding band E(k)=\varepsilon_0-2t\cos ka,\qquad\text{bandwidth }W=4t . Near k=0: E\approx\varepsilon_0-2t+ta^2k^2 ⇒ m^*=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2ta^2}. Weak overlap ⇒ small t ⇒ narrow band ⇒ heavy electrons. Square 2D: E=\varepsilon_0-2t(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya); simple cubic 3D: W=12t.
Two atoms per cell with alternating hoppings t_1,t_2: E_\pm(k)=\pm\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+2t_1t_2\cos ka}, gap 2|t_1-t_2| at k=\pi/a. A two-atom basis doubles the number of bands; dimerization opens a gap.

Nearly free vs tight binding: NFE starts from plane waves and treats V as small (gaps small, bands wide); tight binding starts from atoms and treats overlap as small (bands narrow, gaps big). Real semiconductors sit in between; both give the same qualitative picture: bands, gaps at zone boundaries, v=0 at band extrema.

7. Velocity, effective mass, holes, Bloch oscillations

Semiclassical dynamics v_g=\frac1\hbar\frac{dE}{dk},\qquad\hbar\frac{dk}{dt}=F_{\rm ext}\ (=-eE\text{ for electrons}),\qquad\frac1{m^*}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\ \ \Big(\text{3D: }\big(\tfrac1{m^*}\big)_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac1{\hbar^2}\tfrac{\partial^2E}{\partial k_\mu\partial k_\nu}\Big). Derivation: \frac{dv}{dt}=\frac1\hbar\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\frac{dk}{dt}=\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}F, Newton’s law with mass m^*. Near a minimum E=E_c+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m^*}.   • v=0 at k=0 and k=\pi/a in every band: E(k)=E(-k) makes E'(0)=0; combining with E(k)=E(k+2\pi/a) gives E(\pi/a+\delta)=E(-\pi/a-\delta)=E(\pi/a-\delta), symmetric about \pi/a ⇒ E'(\pi/a)=0. These are standing waves.
  • Negative mass at a band maximum. E=E_v-Bk^2 ⇒ m^*=-\hbar^2/2B: the electron accelerates opposite to the force.
  • Holes. A nearly full band is easier described by its few empty states. A hole has charge +e, mass m_h=-m^*_{\rm electron}>0, wavevector k_h=-k_e, energy measured downward. A full band carries no current; removing an electron at k_e leaves current that behaves like a +e particle.
  • Bloch oscillations. In a uniform field k grows linearly: k(t)=-eEt/\hbar. The electron reaches the zone edge after t=\hbar(\pi/a)/(eE), its velocity having risen then fallen to zero; it is Bragg-reflected to -\pi/a and the cycle repeats, period T_B=h/(eEa). 8. k·p theory: why small gaps mean light electrons

Perturbation theory recap (discussion 4). For \hat H=\hat H_0+\hat H' with known E_n^{(0)},|n\rangle:

E_n\approx E_n^{(0)}+\langle n|\hat H'|n\rangle+\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\langle m|\hat H'|n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}} .

Second order pushes levels apart: a lower state m pushes n up, a higher state pushes it down, more strongly when they are close.

Apply to Bloch functions. Insert \psi=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k} into \hat H\psi=E\psi:

\left[\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V+\frac{\hbar\,\mathbf k\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}}{m}+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\right]u_{n\mathbf k}=E_n(\mathbf k)u_{n\mathbf k}.

At \mathbf k=0 the solutions u_{n0} are known; treat \frac{\hbar}{m}\mathbf k\cdot\hat{\mathbf p} as the perturbation. First order vanishes at a band extremum, and second order gives

k·p effective mass E_n(\mathbf k)=E_n(0)+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}+\frac{\hbar^2}{m^2}\sum_{j\ne n}\frac{|\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf p_{nj}|^2}{E_n-E_j}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac1{m_n^*}=\frac1m+\frac2{m^2}\sum_{j\ne n}\frac{|\langle n|\hat p_x|j\rangle|^2}{E_n-E_j}. Two bands, Kane energy E_P=2|p_{cv}|^2/m\approx20 eV in III–Vs: \frac{m}{m_e}=1+\frac{E_P}{E_g}\approx\frac{E_P}{E_g}\ (E_g\ll E_P),\qquad\frac m{m_e}+\frac m{m_h}=\frac{2E_P}{E_g}. (Lecture’s fuller Kane form with spin–orbit: \frac{m}{m_c^*}\approx1+\frac{E_P}{3}\left(\frac2{E_g}+\frac1{E_g+\Delta_{so}}\right).)

9. Spin in bands: Kramers, inversion, spin–orbit

  • Time reversal (Kramers): E_\uparrow(\mathbf k)=E_\downarrow(-\mathbf k), always (no magnetic field).
  • Inversion symmetry: E_\sigma(\mathbf k)=E_\sigma(-\mathbf k).
  • Both together ⇒ E_\uparrow(\mathbf k)=E_\downarrow(\mathbf k) at every \mathbf k: every band is doubly degenerate, which justifies “2 electrons per k”. Si and Ge (diamond) have inversion; GaAs (zinc blende) does not, so at \mathbf k\ne0 the spin degeneracy can split slightly (Dresselhaus splitting). At \mathbf k=0, Kramers alone gives E_\uparrow(0)=E_\downarrow(0).
  • Spin–orbit coupling \hat H_{so}\propto\vec\sigma\times\nabla V\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}. The valence-band top at Γ comes from p_x,p_y,p_z orbitals: 3\times2=6 states. SO splits them into j=3/2 (4 states: heavy holes m_j=\pm3/2 and light holes \pm1/2, degenerate at Γ) and j=1/2 (2 states, the split-off band, lower by \Delta_{so}). \Delta_{so} grows with atomic number because \nabla V near heavy nuclei is steep: Si 0.044 eV, GaAs 0.34 eV, InSb 0.82 eV.
  • In HgTe, SO is so strong it inverts the bands: a topological insulator (lecture aside).

Counting states and filling them: density of states and Fermi–Dirac

To get any measurable number (carrier density, current, heat capacity) you need two ingredients: how many states exist at each energy, D(E), and the probability each is occupied, f(E). Their product, integrated, is everything. This module builds both from scratch.

1. Counting allowed wavevectors

From the 3D box (Module 2), the allowed wavevectors with hard walls are k_i=n_i\pi/L, n_i=1,2,\dots: a cubic grid of points in the positive octant, one point per volume (\pi/L)^3=\pi^3/V. With periodic (BvK) boundary conditions k_i=2\pi n_i/L, n_i any integer: points fill all of k-space, one per (2\pi/L)^3=8\pi^3/V. Each point holds 2 electrons (spin).

The master count (3D) Number of states with wavevector below k (including spin): N(k)=2\times\frac{\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi k^3}{\pi^3/V}=2\times\frac{\frac43\pi k^3}{8\pi^3/V}=\frac{Vk^3}{3\pi^2}. Hard walls: octant ÷ small cell. Periodic: full sphere ÷ 8× bigger cell. Same answer: for large L bulk properties cannot depend on what happens at the surface.

2. The density of states in 3D

3. Filling the sphere: the Fermi energy

At T=0, N electrons fill the lowest states, one per state. Energy depends only on |\mathbf k|, so the filled region is a sphere (the Fermi sphere) of radius k_F. Set N(k_F)=N:

Free-electron Fermi gas k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3},\qquad T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_B},\qquad v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m}. 5. The Fermi–Dirac distribution, derived by counting

The idea. Group single-particle states into levels: level i has energy E_i and g_i states, holding N_i identical fermions (at most one per state, Pauli). The equilibrium configuration is the one realizable in the most ways.

  1. Ways to put N_i fermions into g_i states: choose which states are filled, Q_i=\binom{g_i}{N_i}=\frac{g_i!}{N_i!(g_i-N_i)!}. Levels are independent ⇒ Q=\prod_iQ_i.
  2. Stirling (\ln x!\approx x\ln x-x): \ln Q=\sum_i[g_i\ln g_i-N_i\ln N_i-(g_i-N_i)\ln(g_i-N_i)] (the -x terms cancel).
  3. Maximize subject to \sum N_i=N and \sum N_iE_i=U with Lagrange multipliers: \frac{\partial\ln Q}{\partial N_i}=\ln\frac{g_i-N_i}{N_i}=\alpha+\beta E_i.
  4. Solve: \frac{N_i}{g_i}=\frac1{e^{\alpha+\beta E_i}+1}. Thermodynamics identifies \beta=1/k_BT, \alpha=-\mu/k_BT.
Fermi–Dirac distribution f(E)=\frac{1}{e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT}+1}\qquad(\mu=E_F\text{ in device language}). f(\mu)=\frac12 at any T. T=0: a step (1 below \mu, 0 above). Symmetry: f(\mu+\delta)=1-f(\mu-\delta). Slope at \mu: -\frac1{4k_BT}. Falls from 0.9 to 0.1 over 2k_BT\ln9=4.4k_BT (0.114 eV at 300 K). Boltzmann tail: f\approx e^{-(E-\mu)/k_BT} when E-\mu\gg k_BT (error 37% at 1k_BT, 5% at 3k_BT).

Hole occupation is 1-f(E): the probability a state is empty. The symmetry says “empty \delta below \mu” is exactly as likely as “filled \delta above \mu”.

Carrier concentration, doping, mobility and the Hall effect

Multiply the density of states by the occupation, integrate, and you get the electron and hole densities. Everything in device physics follows from three results: n=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}, np=n_i^2, and charge neutrality. Then mobility and the Hall effect tell you how to measure n and how fast carriers move.

1. Electron and hole densities

n=\int_{E_c}^{\infty}D_c(E)f(E)\,dE,\qquad p=\int_{-\infty}^{E_v}D_v(E)[1-f(E)]\,dE .

Nondegenerate (dilute) case: if E_c-E_F\gg k_BT and E_F-E_v\gg k_BT (in practice ≳3k_BT), use the Boltzmann tail f\approx e^{-(E-E_F)/k_BT}. Pulling out e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT} and substituting x=(E-E_c)/k_BT:

n=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_c^*k_BT}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}\int_0^\infty\sqrt x\,e^{-x}dx,\qquad\int_0^\infty\sqrt xe^{-x}dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2 .
Effective densities of states n=N_c\,e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT},\quad p=N_v\,e^{-(E_F-E_v)/k_BT},\quad N_{c,v}=2\left(\frac{m^*_{c,v}k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}. N_c(m^*=m_e,300\text{ K})=2.51\times10^{19} cm^{-3}; scale by (m^*/m_e)^{3/2}(T/300)^{3/2}. Si: N_c\approx2.8\times10^{19}, N_v\approx1.05\times10^{19}. GaAs: N_c=4.0\times10^{17}, N_v=9.0\times10^{18} cm^{-3}.
Meaning: as if all CB states were one level at E_c with N_c states. The occupied electrons pile up at E_c+\frac12k_BT (13 meV at 300 K).

2. Mass action and the intrinsic semiconductor

np=N_cN_ve^{-E_g/k_BT}\equiv n_i^2 .

E_F cancels: the product depends only on the material and T, so it is the same in pure and doped material (in equilibrium, nondegenerate). Doping trades electrons for holes; it cannot raise both.

Intrinsic results (n=p=n_i) n_i=\sqrt{N_cN_v}\,e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2k_BT},\qquad E_i=\frac{E_c+E_v}2+\frac{k_BT}2\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c}=\frac{E_c+E_v}{2}+\frac34k_BT\ln\frac{m_v^*}{m_c^*}. n=n_ie^{(E_F-E_i)/k_BT}, p=n_ie^{(E_i-E_F)/k_BT}. Typical 300 K values: Si n_i\approx10^{10} cm^{-3} (formula with E_g=1.12 gives ~7\times10^9; textbooks quote 1.0\times10^{10}), Ge 2\times10^{13}, GaAs 2\times10^6.

E_i sits at midgap at T=0 and moves toward the band with the lighter mass (smaller N) as T rises: the heavier band has more states near its edge, so E_F must move away from it to keep n=p.

3. Doping: donors and acceptors

  • Donor (group V in Si: P, As, Sb): 5 valence electrons, 4 bond, the 5th is loosely bound. Level E_d just below E_c. Neutral when occupied, positive when ionized. Gives n-type.
  • Acceptor (group III in Si: B, Al, Ga, In): grabs an electron to complete bonds, leaving a hole. Level E_a just above E_v. Neutral when empty, negative when ionized. Gives p-type.
  • Amphoteric impurities act either way depending on the site (Si in GaAs: on a Ga site a donor, on an As site an acceptor). Deep impurities (Au, Cu, Fe in Si) sit near midgap and act as recombination centers (Module 8). Vacancies and interstitials can dope too; the NV center in diamond is a defect used for quantum sensing.
Hydrogenic donor model The extra electron orbits the +e ion like hydrogen, but in a medium of dielectric constant \varepsilon_r and with mass m^*: E_d=13.6\text{ eV}\times\frac{m^*/m_e}{\varepsilon_r^2},\qquad a_d=0.529\text{ Å}\times\frac{\varepsilon_r}{m^*/m_e}. GaAs (\varepsilon_r=13.1, m^*=0.067): E_d=5.3 meV, a_d=10 nm, independent of which donor. Measured: Si ≈45 meV, Ge ≈12 meV, GaAs ≈6 meV. Screening (large \varepsilon_r) and light mass make donors shallow, so they are fully ionized at room temperature. The orbit spans thousands of atoms, which is why using m^* and \varepsilon_r is justified.

4. Charge neutrality and where E_F sits

n+N_A^-=p+N_D^+ .

With full ionization and N_D>N_A: combine with np=n_i^2:

n=\frac{N_D-N_A}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{N_D-N_A}2\right)^2+n_i^2}\ \approx N_D-N_A\ \ (\text{if }\gg n_i),\qquad p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}.
Locating the Fermi level n-type: E_c-E_F=k_BT\ln\dfrac{N_c}{N_D}, or E_F-E_i=k_BT\ln\dfrac{n}{n_i}.
p-type: E_F-E_v=k_BT\ln\dfrac{N_v}{N_A}, or E_i-E_F=k_BT\ln\dfrac{p}{n_i}.
Each factor of 10 in doping moves E_F by k_BT\ln10=59.5 meV at 300 K.

Donor occupancy (degeneracy factor 2 from spin): N_D^+=\dfrac{N_D}{1+2e^{(E_F-E_d)/k_BT}}.

5. Temperature dependence of n in a doped sample

  • Freeze-out (low T): k_BT\ll E_c-E_d, donors hold their electrons; n\ll N_D and n\propto e^{-(E_c-E_d)/2k_BT}. Solve neutrality self-consistently with N_D^+.
  • Extrinsic / saturation (mid T, including 300 K): all donors ionized, n\approx N_D, flat.
  • Intrinsic (high T): n_i(T) overtakes N_D and n\approx n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2k_BT}. Plotted as \ln n vs 1/T, the slopes give (E_c-E_d)/2 and E_g/2.

As T rises E_F moves from near E_d (freeze-out) down toward E_i (intrinsic).

6. Valleys, density-of-states mass, conductivity mass

Si’s conduction band has 6 equivalent minima along ⟨100⟩, about 85% of the way to X, each an ellipsoid: E-E_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left(\frac{k_l^2}{m_l}+\frac{k_t^2}{m_t}\right) with m_l=0.98, m_t=0.19. The effective mass becomes a tensor. Two different averages matter:

Two masses for a many-valley band Density-of-states mass (for N_c): m_{dos}=M_c^{2/3}(m_lm_t^2)^{1/3}=6^{2/3}(0.98\times0.19^2)^{1/3}=1.08m_e (this is PS5’s 1.08).
Conductivity mass (for mobility): \frac3{m_{cond}}=\frac1{m_l}+\frac2{m_t} ⇒ m_{cond}=0.26m_e.
GaAs: single isotropic Γ valley (0.067m_e), with heavier L valleys ~0.3 eV higher. Valence bands of all three: heavy hole, light hole, split-off.

Cyclotron resonance measures these masses: \omega_c=eB/m^*, i.e. f=28\text{ GHz/T}\times(m_e/m^*), so microwaves probe semiconductor masses. (The lecture slide prints 82 GHz/T, computed with m_0=3.1\times10^{-31} kg; with m_e=9.109\times10^{-31} kg the free-electron value is eB/2\pi m_e=28 GHz/T. 7. Drift and mobility

Drude model. A carrier feels the field and loses momentum by scattering every \tau on average: m^*\frac{dv}{dt}=qE-\frac{m^*v}{\tau}. In steady state v_d=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}E.

Mobility and conductivity \mu=\frac{v_d}{E}=\frac{q\langle\tau\rangle}{m^*}\ [\text{cm}^2/\text{V s}],\qquad J=nq\mu E,\qquad\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p),\qquad\rho=1/\sigma . Scattering: lattice (phonons) \mu_{ph}\propto T^{-3/2} (more phonons and faster carriers); ionized impurities \mu_I\propto T^{3/2}/(N_D^++N_A^-) (fast carriers deflect less). Combine rates (Matthiessen): \frac1\mu=\frac1{\mu_{ph}}+\frac1{\mu_I}. Typical Si: \mu_n\approx1400, \mu_p\approx450 cm²/V s (lightly doped); GaAs \mu_n\approx8500.

Velocity saturation. At high fields v_d stops growing at v_{sat}\sim10^7 cm/s: once a carrier gains roughly the optical phonon energy (≈60 meV in Si) it emits a phonon and loses it. Estimate: \frac12m^*v^2\approx\hbar\omega_{op} ⇒ v\approx\sqrt{2(0.06\text{ eV})/0.26m_e}\approx3\times10^7 cm/s, the right order (Module 10 ties this to phonons).

8. Diffusion and the Einstein relation

J_n=qn\mu_nE+qD_n\frac{dn}{dx},\qquad J_p=qp\mu_pE-qD_p\frac{dp}{dx},\qquad\frac{D}{\mu}=\frac{k_BT}{q}=25.85\text{ mV at 300 K}.

Electrons diffuse down their gradient; because their charge is negative the current points up the gradient, hence the + sign. Einstein’s relation follows because the same random thermal motion causes both diffusion (D=v_{th}^2\tau/3-type) and resistance to drift (\mu=q\tau/m^*), and m^*v_{th}^2\sim k_BT. A useful length: the Debye length L_D=\sqrt{\varepsilon k_BT/q^2(n_0+p_0)}, how far a charge disturbance is screened.

9. The Hall effect: measuring n and \mu separately

Resistivity gives only the product n\mu. A magnetic field separates them. Current I flows along x in a bar of width W and thickness t; \mathbf B=B\hat z. The Lorentz force q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B pushes carriers sideways until a transverse field E_y cancels it (no current along y in steady state): E_y=v_xB in magnitude.

Hall relations R_H=\frac{E_y}{J_xB}=\frac{1}{qn}\ \ \Big(-\frac1{en}\text{ electrons},\ +\frac1{ep}\text{ holes}\Big),\qquad V_H=\frac{IB}{qnt},\qquad n=\frac{IB}{q\,t\,|V_H|},\qquad\mu=\frac{|R_H|}{\rho}=|R_H|\sigma . The sign of V_H tells the carrier type. In 2D: \rho_{xy}=\frac{B}{en_s} (Ω, independent of size) and \rho_{xx}=\frac{1}{en_s\mu} (Ω/□), so n_s=\frac{B}{e\rho_{xy}}, \mu=\frac{1}{en_s\rho_{xx}}. Two carriers: R_H=\frac{p-nb^2}{q(nb+p)^2} with b=\mu_n/\mu_p.
Drude in a field gives \rho_{xx} independent of B and \rho_{yx} linear in B: this is the low-field classical Hall effect.

Excess carriers, generation and recombination

Shine light on a semiconductor or forward-bias a junction and you push np above n_i^2. Nature pulls it back through three channels: direct radiative recombination (LEDs, lasers), trap-assisted Shockley–Read–Hall recombination (defects, leakage), and Auger recombination (high density). Each sets a lifetime; the fastest one wins.

1. Equilibrium, and what “excess” means

In equilibrium every process is balanced by its reverse (detailed balance): thermal generation G_{th} equals recombination R, and np=n_i^2. Inject carriers (light with h\nu>E_g, or current) and

n=n_0+\Delta n,\qquad p=p_0+\Delta p,\qquad\Delta n=\Delta p\ \text{(pairs are created together)}.

Low-level injection: \Delta n\ll majority density. Then the majority density barely changes while the minority density can change by many orders of magnitude, so the minority carrier controls recombination. High-level injection: \Delta n\gtrsim majority density (lasers, LEDs at high current).

The net recombination rate is always driven by the deviation np-n_i^2: positive means net recombination, negative (e.g. a depletion region where np\lt n_i^2) means net generation.

2. Quasi-Fermi levels

Electrons thermalize among themselves within the conduction band in femtoseconds, and holes within the valence band, much faster than they recombine with each other. So each population is a Fermi–Dirac distribution with its own level:

Quasi-Fermi levels n=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_{Fn})/k_BT}=n_ie^{(E_{Fn}-E_i)/k_BT},\qquad p=N_ve^{-(E_{Fp}-E_v)/k_BT}=n_ie^{(E_i-E_{Fp})/k_BT}, np=n_i^2\,e^{(E_{Fn}-E_{Fp})/k_BT}. In equilibrium E_{Fn}=E_{Fp}=E_F. The splitting E_{Fn}-E_{Fp} measures how far from equilibrium you are (it bounds a solar cell’s open-circuit voltage; a laser needs E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}>h\nu\ge E_g). Under low injection the majority quasi-Fermi level hardly moves; the minority one moves a lot.

3. The continuity equation

\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}=\frac1q\frac{\partial J_n}{\partial x}+G_n-R_n,\qquad\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac1q\frac{\partial J_p}{\partial x}+G_p-R_p .

For a uniform sample (no current divergence) with light generating G_L and recombination written as \Delta n/\tau:

\frac{d\Delta n}{dt}=G_L-\frac{\Delta n}{\tau}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{steady state }\Delta n=G_L\tau;\qquad\text{light off: }\Delta n(t)=\Delta n(0)e^{-t/\tau}.

Photoconductivity decay (lecture experiment): a light pulse raises the conductivity by \Delta\sigma=q(\mu_n+\mu_p)\Delta n; after the pulse \Delta\sigma decays as e^{-t/\tau}, so the decay time is the minority-carrier lifetime. Short lifetime ⇒ many defects: the measurement doubles as a material-quality test.

4. Direct (radiative) band-to-band recombination

An electron drops from the CB into an empty VB state and emits a photon of energy ≈ E_g. The rate needs an electron and a hole: R=Bnp. In equilibrium thermal generation balances it, G_{th}=Bn_0p_0=Bn_i^2 (answer to the slide’s question “what is the generation rate in the absence of light?”). Net:

Radiative lifetime U_{rad}=B(np-n_i^2)=B\,\Delta n\,(n_0+p_0+\Delta n)\ \Rightarrow\ \tau_{rad}=\frac{1}{B(n_0+p_0+\Delta n)}\ \xrightarrow{\text{n-type, low inj.}}\ \frac1{Bn_0}. B at 300 K: GaAs 7.2\times10^{-10}, Ge 5.3\times10^{-14}, Si 1.8\times10^{-15} cm³/s.

Why B differs by 10^5 between GaAs and Si: direct vs indirect gaps. A photon carries almost no momentum on the scale of the BZ (k_{photon}=2\pi/\lambda\sim10^{-3} Å^{-1} vs zone size ~1 Å^{-1}), so optical transitions are vertical in E–k. In GaAs the CB minimum and VB maximum are both at Γ: direct, fast, efficient light emission (LEDs, lasers). In Si the CB minimum is near X: the electron must also emit or absorb a phonon to change k, a second-order, much slower process. That is why light emitters use direct-gap III–Vs.

At high injection (\Delta n\gg n_0,p_0): \frac{d\Delta n}{dt}=-B\Delta n^2, so the decay is not exponential: \frac1{\Delta n}=\frac1{\Delta n_0}+Bt.

5. Shockley–Read–Hall: recombination through a trap

A defect level E_t in the gap (density N_t) recombines carriers in two steps: it captures an electron, then a hole (or the reverse). Four processes, with f_t the fraction of traps holding an electron:

ProcessNeedsRate
(a) electron captureempty trapr_a=c_nnN_t(1-f_t), c_n=\sigma_nv_{th}
(b) electron emissionfull trapr_b=e_nN_tf_t
(c) hole capturefull trapr_c=c_ppN_tf_t, c_p=\sigma_pv_{th}
(d) hole emissionempty trapr_d=e_pN_t(1-f_t)

Capture coefficient: a trap “sweeps” a volume \sigma v_{th} per second (v_{th}=\sqrt{3k_BT/m^*}\approx10^7 cm/s), so c_n=\sigma_nv_{th} (cm³/s).

  1. Detailed balance in equilibrium (r_a=r_b with the equilibrium f_t) gives the emission rates: e_n=c_nn_1, e_p=c_pp_1, where n_1=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_t)/k_BT}=n_ie^{(E_t-E_i)/k_BT} and p_1=n_ie^{(E_i-E_t)/k_BT}: the n and p you would have if E_F sat at E_t. Note n_1p_1=n_i^2.
  2. Steady state: electron and hole net capture must be equal (traps don’t pile up charge): r_a-r_b=r_c-r_d ⇒ f_t=\dfrac{c_nn+e_p}{c_nn+e_n+c_pp+e_p}.
  3. Substitute:
SRH rate U_{SRH}=\frac{np-n_i^2}{\tau_{p0}(n+n_1)+\tau_{n0}(p+p_1)},\qquad\tau_{n0}=\frac1{\sigma_nv_{th}N_t},\ \ \tau_{p0}=\frac1{\sigma_pv_{th}N_t}. Equal lifetimes \tau_0: U=\dfrac{np-n_i^2}{\tau_0\left[n+p+2n_i\cosh\frac{E_t-E_i}{k_BT}\right]}.
• Low injection, n-type: U\approx\Delta p/\tau_{p0}, the minority lifetime. More traps ⇒ shorter lifetime (\tau\propto1/N_t).
• The cosh is smallest at E_t=E_i: midgap traps are the most effective recombination and generation centers.
• Where np\ll n_i^2 (depletion region): net generation G=\dfrac{n_i}{2\tau_0\cosh[(E_t-E_i)/k_BT]}: the source of reverse leakage current.

Where traps come from. (1) Impurities: Au, Pt, Cu, Fe in Si give deep levels; Au/Pt are added on purpose to kill lifetime in fast-switching diodes. (2) Radiation and processing damage: vacancies and interstitials (can anneal out). (3) Surfaces: dangling bonds, ~10^{14} cm^{-2} on a bare surface, 10^{10}–10^{12} under thermal oxide (one reason SiO₂ made Si win). Surface recombination is described by a velocity s (cm/s): U_s=s\,\Delta p per unit area.

6. Auger recombination

Three particles: an electron and hole recombine and give their energy (≈E_g) to a third carrier, which is kicked high into its band and then relaxes by emitting phonons (heat). Non-radiative. Needs a high density of the third carrier.

Auger rate and lifetime U_{A}=(C_nn+C_pp)(np-n_i^2)\ \Rightarrow\ \tau_{A}^{-1}=C_n(n_0+\Delta n)^2\approx C_nn^2\ \ (\text{n-type}). C\sim10^{-31}–10^{-30} cm⁶/s in Si (e.g. C_n\approx2.8\times10^{-31}). Because \tau_A\propto1/n^2, Auger dominates in heavily doped regions and at high injection (lasers, LED efficiency droop).

7. Putting the lifetimes together

\frac1\tau=\frac1{\tau_{SRH}}+\frac1{\tau_{rad}}+\frac1{\tau_{Auger}}\qquad(\text{rates add; the fastest process dominates}).

Versus majority density in n-Si: flat SRH-limited lifetime at low doping, then a 1/N radiative region (weak in Si), then a steep 1/N^2 Auger drop above ~10^{18} cm^{-3}. In GaAs radiative recombination is strong enough to dominate over a wide range: good for light emission.

Starting from the four rates in the table: (a) Use detailed balance in equilibrium to show e_n=c_nn_1 with n_1=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_t)/k_BT}. (b) Find the steady-state f_t. (c) Show U=r_a-r_b=\frac{N_tc_nc_p(np-n_1p_1)}{c_n(n+n_1)+c_p(p+p_1)} and that n_1p_1=n_i^2.

(a) In equilibrium f_t=1/(1+e^{(E_t-E_F)/k_BT}), so \frac{1-f_t}{f_t}=e^{(E_t-E_F)/k_BT}; combine with n_0=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}.

(a) c_nn_0N_t(1-f_t)=e_nN_tf_t ⇒ e_n=c_nn_0\frac{1-f_t}{f_t}=c_nN_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}e^{(E_t-E_F)/k_BT}=c_nN_ce^{-(E_c-E_t)/k_BT}=c_nn_1. E_F drops out, as it must for a property of the trap. Likewise e_p=c_pp_1.

(b) r_a+r_d=r_b+r_c: c_nn(1-f_t)+e_p(1-f_t)=e_nf_t+c_ppf_t ⇒ f_t=\frac{c_nn+e_p}{c_nn+e_n+c_pp+e_p}.

(c) U=N_t[c_nn(1-f_t)-e_nf_t]. With 1-f_t=\frac{e_n+c_pp}{D}, D the denominator: U=\frac{N_t}{D}[c_nn(e_n+c_pp)-e_n(c_nn+e_p)]=\frac{N_t}{D}[c_nc_pnp-e_ne_p]=\frac{N_tc_nc_p(np-n_1p_1)}{c_n(n+n_1)+c_p(p+p_1)}. n_1p_1=N_cN_ve^{-(E_c-E_v)/k_BT}=n_i^2. Divide by N_tc_nc_p to get the boxed form.

Homojunctions, heterojunctions and band engineering

Join two different semiconductors and you can place the conduction and valence band edges wherever you want in space: wells that trap electrons, barriers that block them, layers that emit a chosen color. The physics is short; the exam skills are reading the gap–lattice-constant chart, Vegard’s law, band offsets, and drawing band diagrams.

1. What a heterojunction is, and why build one

A homojunction (ordinary pn junction) has the same semiconductor on both sides; only the doping changes. A heterojunction is an atomically sharp interface (one or two monolayers) between two different semiconductors, grown epitaxially by MBE or MOCVD. Because the gaps differ, the band edges jump at the interface. With layers like GaAs/AlGaAs/GaAs you can build quantum wells, barriers, superlattices, modulation-doped channels (HEMTs), heterojunction bipolar transistors, LEDs and lasers.

pn homojunction reminder. In equilibrium E_F is flat; the bands bend across a depletion region by the built-in potential

qV_{bi}=k_BT\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}\quad(\text{Si},\ N_A=N_D=10^{16}:\ V_{bi}=0.714\text{ V}).

Charge is screened over roughly the Debye length L_D=\sqrt{\varepsilon k_BT/q^2(n_0+p_0)}.

2. Lattice matching and Vegard’s law

Good epitaxy needs nearly the same lattice constant; otherwise the interface fills with misfit dislocations that act as recombination centers (Module 8). The standard chart plots band gap (vertical) against lattice constant (horizontal). Materials on the same vertical line can be grown on each other.

Vegard’s law (ternary alloy A_xB_{1-x}C) a(x)=x\,a_{AC}+(1-x)\,a_{BC}. Key matches: Al_xGa_{1-x}As on GaAs for any x (AlAs 5.661 Å vs GaAs 5.653 Å); In_{0.53}Ga_{0.47}As on InP (5.869 Å); Ga_{0.5}In_{0.5}P on GaAs. The gap varies smoothly with x (with some bowing): AlGaAs runs from 1.42 eV (GaAs, direct) to 2.16 eV (AlAs, indirect), crossing from direct to indirect near x\approx0.45.

On the chart, the more alloy lines that cross a substrate’s vertical line, the more that material system can be engineered.

3. Strained layers and critical thickness

Mismatch f=\frac{a_{layer}-a_{sub}}{a_{sub}}. A thin enough layer stretches or squeezes in-plane to match the substrate (pseudomorphic, strained) with no defects. Above a critical thickness it becomes cheaper to form misfit dislocations and the layer relaxes, ruining the interface. Thicker layer, or larger mismatch, ⇒ more likely to relax.

SiGe on Si: Si/Ge mismatch ≈ 4.2% (slides say ~4.5%). Critical thickness ranges from ~10 nm to 1 μm depending on Ge fraction. For a 0.1 μm HBT base, keep Ge ≲ 15% (≈1% mismatch at most), giving \Delta E_v\approx100 meV. Strain also changes band structure (strained Si boosts mobility in CMOS since the 90 nm node).

4. Band offsets and alignment types

Basic identity \Delta E_g=E_{g1}-E_{g2}=\Delta E_c+\Delta E_v . The total is known precisely; how it splits between CB and VB is measured, not derived. AlGaAs/GaAs (x\lt0.4): \Delta E_c/\Delta E_g\approx0.62. Lecture example: gaps 1.91 and 1.42 eV ⇒ \Delta E_g=0.49, \Delta E_c=0.30, \Delta E_v=0.19 eV. Offsets do not depend on doping.

5. Drawing a heterojunction band diagram

  1. Draw each material’s bulk bands far from the interface, with E_F placed by its doping (E_c-E_F=k_BT\ln(N_c/N_D) etc.).
  2. In equilibrium E_F is a single flat line across the whole structure.
  3. At the interface, put in the fixed steps \Delta E_c and \Delta E_v.
  4. Bend the bands smoothly in between (depletion/accumulation regions) so that they join the bulk bands; on each side E_c and E_v stay parallel, separated by that side’s E_g.

Flat-band condition. If the dopings are chosen so that the two E_F’s already line up, there is no band bending and the CB is a clean rectangular step of height \Delta E_c. Wrong doping gives severe bending and non-rectangular barriers (the BandProf simulations on the slides).

Modulation doping (HEMT): dope only the wide-gap AlGaAs. Its electrons fall across the interface into the lower CB of undoped GaAs and collect in a thin triangular well: a 2D electron gas (Module 6) spatially separated from the ionized donors, so impurity scattering is weak and mobility is very high.

6. Quantum wells, LEDs and lasers

  • Quantum well: small-gap layer between large-gap barriers (type I). Confinement adds energy: emission at E_g+E_{1e}+E_{1h} (Module 2 well energies), so thinner wells emit bluer light. Electrons and holes are held together, raising radiative recombination.
  • LED: forward-biased junction injects electrons and holes into the same region; they recombine radiatively (direct gap needed, Module 8). \lambda[\text{nm}]=1240/E_g[\text{eV}]. The smaller-gap material sets the color. Blue needed GaN (3.4 eV) / InGaN, which is why blue LEDs came late.
  • Laser: needs population inversion (many electrons in the CB opposite many holes in the VB, E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}>h\nu), achieved with heavily doped p⁺n⁺ or a double heterostructure under strong forward bias, plus mirrors for optical feedback. Stimulated photons are coherent with the stimulating ones (Einstein, 1916).
  • Valleys matter: in GaAs the Γ valley has m^*=0.067 (high mobility) while the L/X valleys ~0.3 eV higher are heavier; hot electrons scattered into them slow down. InAs has very light electrons but a tiny gap (0.36 eV), so leakage is a problem.

Phonons: lattice vibrations, their quanta, heat capacity and heat flow

The atoms are not frozen on their lattice sites; they are masses joined by springs. Solving those springs uses the same Bloch logic as electrons (wavevectors in a Brillouin zone, dispersion relations, group velocity). Quantizing them uses the harmonic oscillator from Module 2. The result explains heat capacity, thermal conductivity, and why electrons scatter and saturate.

1. The monatomic chain

Near equilibrium the interatomic potential is a parabola, so each bond is a spring (Hooke: F=-Cx). Atoms of mass M at x_s=sa, displacements u_s, nearest-neighbor springs C:

M\frac{d^2u_s}{dt^2}=C(u_{s+1}-u_s)+C(u_{s-1}-u_s)=C(u_{s+1}+u_{s-1}-2u_s).

Try a wave u_s=u\,e^{i(sqa-\omega t)}. Every term carries e^{isqa}, which cancels:

-M\omega^2=C(e^{iqa}+e^{-iqa}-2)=-2C(1-\cos qa)=-4C\sin^2\frac{qa}2 .
Monatomic chain results \omega(q)=2\sqrt{\frac CM}\left|\sin\frac{qa}2\right|,\qquad v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dq}=a\sqrt{\frac CM}\cos\frac{qa}2,\qquad -\frac\pi a\lt q\le\frac\pi a . Long wavelength (qa\ll1): \omega\approx a\sqrt{C/M}\,q, a sound wave with v_s=a\sqrt{C/M}. Zone edge (q=\pi/a): maximum \omega_{max}=2\sqrt{C/M}, v_g=0, neighbors move exactly out of phase (u_{s+1}/u_s=e^{i\pi}=-1, \lambda=2a): a standing wave, Bragg-reflected, the same physics as electrons at the zone edge.

Why only the first BZ. The ratio u_{s+1}/u_s=e^{iqa} is unchanged by q\to q+2\pi n/a: a wave with q outside the zone describes exactly the same atomic motion as one inside (there is nothing between the atoms to wiggle). With N atoms on a ring, q=2\pi j/Na: exactly N modes, one per degree of freedom. A uniform shift costs no energy, guaranteeing an acoustic mode with \omega(0)=0. Phonon crystal momentum \hbar q is defined modulo \hbar G.

2. The continuum (sound-wave) limit and 3D polarizations

For qa\ll1, u_{s+1}+u_{s-1}-2u_s\approx a^2\partial^2u/\partial x^2 and the chain becomes the elastic wave equation \rho\,\partial_t^2u=C_{11}\partial_x^2u, so v_L=\sqrt{C_{11}/\rho}. In 3D every \mathbf q has 3 polarizations: 1 longitudinal (atoms move along \mathbf q, compression) and 2 transverse (shear). Isotropic solid: c_L=\sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho}, c_T=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}, so c_L>c_T. Silicon along [100]: v_L=\sqrt{165.7\text{ GPa}/2329}=8435 m/s, v_T=\sqrt{79.6\text{ GPa}/2329}=5846 m/s; at q=10^8 m^{-1} these give 134 GHz and 93 GHz. Stiffer bonds raise sound speed; heavier atoms lower it. The continuum picture cannot describe the zone boundary.

3. Two atoms per primitive cell: acoustic and optical branches

Masses M_1 and M_2 alternate, springs C, repeat distance a (displacements u_s, v_s):

M_1\ddot u_s=C(v_s+v_{s-1}-2u_s),\qquad M_2\ddot v_s=C(u_{s+1}+u_s-2v_s).

Plane-wave trial solutions give a 2×2 determinant whose roots are

Diatomic chain \omega^2=C\left(\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}\right)\pm C\sqrt{\left(\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}\right)^2-\frac{4\sin^2(qa/2)}{M_1M_2}} . q\to0: optical \omega_+=\sqrt{2C\left(\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}\right)}=\sqrt{2C/\mu} (\mu = reduced mass), with u/v=-M_2/M_1 (atoms move oppositely; the heavier moves less; the center of mass stays put). Acoustic \omega_-\approx\sqrt{\frac{C}{2(M_1+M_2)}}\,qa with u=v (the cell moves as a whole).
q=\pi/a: \omega=\sqrt{2C/M_1} and \sqrt{2C/M_2} (for M_1>M_2, only the heavy atoms move in the lower mode, only the light ones in the upper): a frequency gap where no waves propagate. Equal masses close the gap and the true period halves.

Why the optical frequency stays finite at q=0: even when every cell has the same phase, the two atoms in each cell move against each other and stretch their bonds, so a restoring force remains. In an ionic crystal that relative motion is an oscillating dipole \mathbf p=Z^*\mathbf w that couples to light (infrared absorption): hence “optical”. Light has tiny momentum, so it probes only q\approx0 (Γ). Infrared needs a changing dipole, Raman a changing polarizability; Si’s optical phonon (~64 meV, 15.5 THz, the famous 520 cm^{-1} Raman line) is Raman-active but not IR-active because both atoms are identical. Optical phonons are typically >10 THz.

4. Counting branches and modes

p atoms per primitive cell, N cells, 3D 3pN degrees of freedom ⇒ 3p branches, each with N allowed q in the BZ.
Acoustic: 3 branches (1 LA + 2 TA), 3N modes. Optical: 3p-3 branches (p-1 LO + 2p-2 TO), (3p-3)N modes.
Cu, Cr (p=1): 3 acoustic, 0 optical. Si, Ge, GaAs, NaCl (p=2): 3 + 3 = 6 branches. Mn₃Ge (p=3): 3 + 6. A 1D chain with 2 atoms: 1 + 1. Different atomic species are not required for optical branches (Si has them).

Flat parts of a dispersion curve far from Γ contribute many states per frequency interval: you can eyeball the phonon density of states from where the branches flatten.

5. Quantization: phonons

Fourier transforming the chain Hamiltonian H=\sum\frac{p_s^2}{2M}+\frac C2\sum(u_{s+1}-u_s)^2 into normal coordinates Q_q,P_q decouples it into independent oscillators: H=\sum_q\left[\frac{P_qP_{-q}}{2M}+\frac12M\omega_q^2Q_qQ_{-q}\right] with the same \omega_q as the classical dispersion. Each mode is a quantum harmonic oscillator (Module 2):

Phonons E=\sum_{\mathbf q,s}\left(n_{\mathbf qs}+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega_{\mathbf qs},\qquad\langle n\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}\ \ (\text{Planck / Bose–Einstein}). A phonon is one quantum \hbar\omega of a mode; n_{\mathbf q} counts phonons in it; ladder operators \hat a^\dagger,\hat a create and destroy them. Phonons are bosons (any number per mode), unlike electrons.

Phonon momentum in scattering. Elastic X-ray scattering: \mathbf k'=\mathbf k+\mathbf G. Inelastic, creating a phonon \mathbf q: \mathbf k'+\mathbf q=\mathbf k+\mathbf G (the photon loses \hbar\omega_q). Absorbing a phonon: \mathbf k'-\mathbf q=\mathbf k+\mathbf G. Neutron and X-ray inelastic scattering map \omega(\mathbf q) this way.

Link to electrons: an electron that gains about one optical-phonon energy (~60 meV in Si) emits a phonon and loses it; that is the microscopic origin of velocity saturation (Module 7). Phonon scattering also gives \mu_{ph}\propto T^{-3/2}.

6. Heat capacity: from Dulong–Petit to Debye

Classically each atom contributes 3k_B (equipartition): C_V=3Nk_B=3R=24.9 J/mol K (Dulong–Petit), independent of T. Measurements agree at high T but C_V\to0 as T\to0. Reason: a mode with \hbar\omega\gg k_BT is frozen out (\langle n\rangle\approx0).

Debye model. Treat all branches as sound waves \omega=v_sq. Counting modes exactly as for electrons (Module 6, but one state per \mathbf q per polarization): N(q)=\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac43\pi q^3 ⇒ D(\omega)=\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2v_s^3} per polarization. Cut off at \omega_D so the total equals N modes per polarization:

Debye results \omega_D=v_s\left(\frac{6\pi^2N}{V}\right)^{1/3},\quad\theta_D=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B},\quad U=9Nk_BT\left(\frac T{\theta_D}\right)^3\int_0^{\theta_D/T}\frac{x^3dx}{e^x-1}. T\gg\theta_D: C_V\to3Nk_B. T\ll\theta_D: C_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac T{\theta_D}\right)^3 (the T^3 law). The cutoff depends only on the atom density, not sample size. Diamond has \theta_D\approx2230 K, so at room temperature it is far below its classical heat capacity (stiff, light atoms).

Einstein’s simpler model (one frequency for all modes) gets the high-T limit and the freeze-out, but predicts an exponential drop at low T instead of T^3 because it misses the low-frequency acoustic modes.

7. Thermal conductivity and Umklapp scattering

Heat flux j_U=-\kappa\,dT/dx. Phonons carry energy \hbar\omega at v_g and diffuse between collisions. Kinetic theory (same argument as for a gas; the \frac13 is the 3D angular average):

Phonon thermal conductivity \kappa=\frac13C_Vv\ell=\frac13C_Vv^2\tau,\qquad\ell=v\tau . C_V per unit volume, v an average group velocity (acoustic phonons carry most heat), \ell the mean free path. Room temperature: diamond ~2000, SiC ~400, Si ~150, GaN ~130–230, GaAs ~50 W/m K.

Normal vs Umklapp collisions. In a three-phonon collision energy is conserved, \hbar\omega_1+\hbar\omega_2=\hbar\omega_3, and crystal momentum up to \mathbf G: \mathbf q_1+\mathbf q_2=\mathbf q_3+\mathbf G. Normal (\mathbf G=0) collisions conserve the total phonon momentum, so they cannot stop a drifting heat current. Umklapp (“flip-over”, \mathbf G\ne0) collisions, where \mathbf q_1+\mathbf q_2 lands outside the BZ and folds back reversed, destroy phonon momentum and create thermal resistance. They need phonons with q near the zone edge, whose population \sim e^{-\theta_D/bT} grows with T; so at high T \kappa falls (~1/T), while at low T \kappa\propto C_V\propto T^3, limited by sample boundaries and defects.

  2. Reporting \theta instead of 2\theta for a diffraction peak.
